What a Decade of POCs Taught Me About Buying AI

By Nico Castagnola · August 2026 · 6 min read

For ten years, my job has been standing in front of skeptical buyers while they decided whether my company’s software was real. Not whether the demo was impressive—demos are always impressive; that’s what demos are for. Whether it was real: on their data, in their environment, at their volume, handled by their people.

The mechanism enterprises use to answer that question is the proof of concept. Before a serious buyer commits serious money, they make the vendor prove the product on a slice of the buyer’s actual problem, against criteria written down in advance, on a clock. I’ve run hundreds of these evaluations from the vendor side. I have watched deals die because a product that demoed beautifully couldn’t survive contact with real invoices, real tickets, real edge cases.

Here is what strikes me now, watching growing businesses buy AI automation: almost nobody makes AI vendors and consultants earn it the way enterprise buyers made us earn it. The typical AI engagement starts with a vision deck and a retainer, and ends—months later—with something that quietly doesn’t get used. The failure isn’t the technology. It’s the buying process.

What the POC discipline actually is

Strip away the enterprise procurement theater and a good POC has four parts:

1. Success criteria, in writing, before anything is built. Not “we’ll know it when we see it.” Numbers: the invoice takes 22 minutes to process today; the automation must get it under 2, with an error rate below 3%. If you can’t write the criteria, you don’t understand the problem yet—which is itself worth learning before you spend.

2. Real data, real work. Every product works on the demo dataset. The demo dataset was chosen because the product works on it. The only test that matters runs on your messy, inconsistent, exception-riddled reality.

3. A clock. Open-ended evaluations don’t conclude; they dissolve. Thirty days is enough to prove almost any well-scoped automation. If someone needs six months to show value, the scope is wrong.

4. A decision rule. Pass means scale. Miss means stop. Agreed in advance, so nobody relitigates it under the influence of sunk costs and enthusiasm.

Why AI sellers avoid this structure

Because it’s bad for them and good for you. A consultant paid monthly to “drive AI transformation” has no incentive to reach a pass/fail moment—the ambiguity is the business model. A structure with written criteria and a day-30 verdict transfers risk from the buyer to the seller. That’s exactly why you should insist on it, and why a seller’s reaction to the request is itself a signal. The ones who flinch were planning to sell you confidence. The ones who say “great, here’s how I’d structure the criteria” have done this before.

The questions to ask anyone selling you automation

You don’t need to be technical to run this gauntlet. Ask these, and listen for numbers in the answers:

“What will we measure, and what’s the baseline today?” — If they haven’t asked to observe your current process, they can’t answer this honestly.

“What happens if the pilot misses?” — The right answer involves you stopping, keeping the work products, and understanding why. The wrong answer is a pivot to how iteration is part of the journey.

“Will it run on our real data during the pilot?” — A pilot on sample data is a demo with a longer invoice.

“Who maintains this in month six?” — Automations aren’t paintings; they’re plumbing. Formats drift, APIs change, volumes grow. Someone must own that, and the answer should be written into the deal.

The part nobody tells you: failed pilots are a win

In enterprise evaluations, a POC that fails fast is a success—it just saved the buyer a seven-figure mistake at a five-figure price. The same math holds at SMB scale. A 30-day pilot that proves your invoice process isn’t ready for automation (usually because the upstream data is chaos) is cheap tuition, and it tells you exactly what to fix first. The expensive version of that lesson is the same discovery in month eight of a retainer.

The AI wave is real. The leverage for growing businesses is real—I’ve never seen technology this well-suited to the manual glue work that eats small teams alive. But real technology deserves a real buying process. Demand proof. The sellers worth hiring will thank you for it.
