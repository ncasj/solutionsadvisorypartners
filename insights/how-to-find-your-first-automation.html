How to Find Your First Automation (Before You Buy Any Tools)

By Nico Castagnola · August 2026 · 7 min read

The most expensive mistake in automation isn’t choosing the wrong tool. It’s choosing the wrong process—spending real money automating something rare, ambiguous, or low-stakes while the actual time-sink keeps burning hours in plain sight. Tool selection is a downstream decision. Process selection is the one that determines whether automation pays back at all.

Here’s the method I use in every audit. You can run a rough version of it yourself this week, with a spreadsheet and zero new software.

Step 1: Inventory the manual work (follow three trails)

Nobody can list their manual processes from memory—the work is invisible precisely because it’s routine. So instead of asking “what should we automate?”, follow three trails:

Follow the copy-paste. Wherever someone moves information from one place to another—email to CRM, PDF to spreadsheet, spreadsheet to invoice—there’s a process worth writing down. Ask each person: “what do you retype, and how often?”

Follow the owner’s inbox. Whatever routinely waits on the founder or a manager—approvals, quotes, client answers, report sign-offs—is a bottleneck candidate. Bottleneck automations often pay back more than time-saver automations because they unblock other people.

Follow the busy-week casualties. What slipped last time things got hectic? Follow-ups, invoices, status updates? Anything that fails under load is a process running on human memory, and it’s usually the cheapest thing to make bulletproof.

You’ll end up with 15–30 items. That’s normal, and it’s already more clarity than most businesses ever get.

Step 2: Score each one, roughly

For every item, estimate four things. Precision doesn’t matter; order of magnitude does.

Frequency — how many times per week does this happen?
Time — minutes per occurrence, honestly (include the context-switching).
Error cost — what happens when it’s done wrong or late? Annoyance, or a lost client?
Judgment content — how much of the task is genuine human judgment vs. reading, moving, formatting, and drafting?

Multiply frequency by time and you have weekly hours. Multiply by what those hours cost you loaded, and manual work stops being a vibe and becomes a number. A task that takes 20 minutes, 15 times a week, is 260 hours a year. At even $50/hour loaded, that single row is a $13,000 line item nobody ever budgeted.

Step 3: Pick for provability, not impressiveness

The best first automation is almost never the most exciting one. It has a specific profile:

High volume, low judgment. Lots of repetitions, most of them routine. The judgment-heavy 10% stays with a person via a review queue—that’s a feature, not a compromise.

Clear input, clear output. “Invoice PDF arrives → coded entry in accounting system” is automatable. “Keep clients happy” is not a process; it’s a goal hiding several processes.

Measurable today. You need a baseline to prove improvement. If you can’t say how long the task takes now, spend a week measuring before you build anything.

Internal first. For automation number one, prefer a process where a mistake is caught internally over one where a mistake lands in a customer’s inbox. Earn trust with the system before you point it outward.

And two traps to avoid: don’t start with the rare-but-painful process (quarterly board pack agony is real, but a 4×-a-year process gives you almost no repetitions to learn from), and don’t start with the process nobody can describe consistently. If three people do it three ways, standardize first—that’s free—then automate the standard.

Step 4: Write the success criteria before anyone builds

Take your winner and write three numbers: the baseline (what it costs today), the target (what the automation must achieve), and the error tolerance (what exception rate is acceptable, and who catches them). That one-page document does two jobs: it forces the process to be understood before money moves, and it turns your eventual pilot—whoever runs it—into something with a pass/fail verdict instead of a vibes-based extension.

This is exactly the discipline enterprise buyers impose on software vendors, scaled down to fit a growing business. It’s also, not coincidentally, the first two weeks of every Proofworks engagement. But the method isn’t proprietary—run it yourself, and even if you never hire anyone, you’ll know precisely where your week is going and what getting it back is worth.
