Ten Automations a Growing Business Can Ship in 30 Days

By Nico Castagnola · August 2026 · 8 min read

When people imagine AI automation, they imagine the moonshot: an autonomous agent running a department. What actually pays back, over and over, is far less cinematic—the high-volume glue work that connects your systems and eats your team’s week twenty minutes at a time.

These are ten automations I consider proven at the 10–200 person scale. Each is genuinely shippable in a 30-day pilot. For each: what it does, why it pays, and—because honesty is the brand—where it gets hard.

1. Lead intake, enrichment & routing

Every inbound lead—form fill, email, referral—gets enriched with company data, scored against your fit criteria, logged in the CRM, routed to the right person, and answered with a drafted (human-approved) first reply. Speed-to-lead is one of the best-documented revenue levers there is, and most businesses measure theirs in days. Where it gets hard: defining fit criteria you actually believe; garbage scoring erodes trust in week two.

2. Call notes → CRM

Sales and client calls transcribed, summarized, and written into the correct CRM fields, with follow-up tasks created and the next email drafted. Kills the daily “update the CRM” hour and—the bigger prize—makes your pipeline data real for the first time. Where it gets hard: mapping summaries to your fields and stages; a generic transcript dump helps nobody.

3. Proposal & quote first drafts

The automation drafts proposals from call context, your service catalog, and your best past proposals; a human reviews, adjusts pricing judgment, and sends. Three-day turnarounds become same-day. Where it gets hard: if your past proposals are inconsistent, the automation learns your inconsistency—curate the source library first.

4. Client onboarding orchestration

A signed contract triggers everything: accounts created, folders and checklists spun up, kickoff scheduled, welcome sequence sent, internal handoff brief drafted. The first-week experience becomes identical for every client, which is what “professional” actually looks like from the outside. Where it gets hard: agreeing internally on what the standard onboarding is. The automation forces the conversation—that’s a feature.

5. Invoice processing & AP coding

Incoming invoices read regardless of format, coded to the right categories, matched against expectations, and queued for one-click approval—with anything unusual routed to a human. Twenty minutes each becomes two, and month-end stops being archaeology. Where it gets hard: vendor format drift; budget for a parser that learns new templates, and keep the exception queue honest.

6. Collections follow-up

Overdue invoices chased automatically with polite, escalating, personalized sequences; payments reconciled; only genuine disputes reach a person. Most businesses are owed meaningful money simply because chasing it is awkward and forgettable. Where it gets hard: tone. The sequences must sound like you on your best-mannered day, because this automation emails your customers.

7. Monthly reporting packs

Board packs, client reports, or management dashboards assembled from your live systems into your template, as a draft a human annotates. Two days of copy-paste becomes an hour of actual thinking. Where it gets hard: if the underlying data is wrong, the automation industrializes wrongness. A data-hygiene pass usually comes first—which is why this is rarely automation number one.

8. Shared-inbox triage

The info@ / support@ inbox classified, routed, and answered-in-draft, grounded in your real documentation, with a person approving sends. Response times collapse; nothing sits unanswered over a weekend. Where it gets hard: your documentation is probably thinner than you think. The pilot usually reveals the ten answers that cover 60% of volume—write those well and the automation flies.

9. Sales-to-delivery handoff

Everything promised during the sale—scope, commitments, pricing quirks, stakeholder context—compiled automatically into a brief for whoever delivers. The gap between what was sold and what gets delivered is where churn is born; this closes it. Where it gets hard: it depends on automation #2 existing, so the promises are captured somewhere structured in the first place.

10. Renewal & account-review prep

Before every renewal or QBR, usage data, support history, open items, and relationship notes compiled into an account summary. Your team walks in prepared instead of reconstructing; quiet at-risk accounts get flagged while there’s time to act. Where it gets hard: pulling from many systems means many integrations—start with the two sources that matter most.

How to read this list

Notice the pattern: every one of these has high volume, a clear input and output, and a human review point where judgment or customer contact lives. That’s not a coincidence—it’s the profile of automation that survives month six. And no business should build all ten at once. The right move is an audit to find which one pays back fastest for your specific mess, then a 30-day pilot with written success criteria to prove it. One proven automation that your team trusts beats five impressive ones they’ve quietly stopped using.
