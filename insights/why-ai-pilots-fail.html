Why AI Pilots Fail—and How to Write Success Criteria That Mean Something

By Nico Castagnola · August 2026 · 6 min read

Most AI pilots don’t fail loudly. There’s no dramatic postmortem, no line item labeled “the automation that didn’t work.” They dissolve: usage tapers, the champion changes jobs or priorities, the tool quietly stops being opened, and six months later someone says “we tried AI for that, it didn’t really stick.” The money is gone, and—worse—the organization has learned the wrong lesson: automation doesn’t work for us.

Having run technical evaluations professionally for a decade, I can tell you the dissolution is almost never about the technology. It’s about structure. Here are the failure modes I see repeatedly, and the one-page document that prevents all of them.

The five ways pilots dissolve

1. No baseline. Nobody measured what the process cost before the pilot, so nobody can demonstrate what changed. “It feels faster” doesn’t survive a budget conversation. If you remember one thing: measure the current state for a week before anything gets built.

2. Demo data instead of real work. The pilot runs on clean examples and works beautifully. Then real volume arrives with its malformed PDFs, weird edge cases, and that one client who emails photos of documents. A pilot that doesn’t face the mess is a demo with a longer timeline.

3. No decision date. Open-ended pilots never conclude—they just age. Without a scheduled day when someone reads the numbers and decides, the pilot’s natural end state is limbo.

4. Success by enthusiasm. The verdict gets decided by how excited people feel in week one, not what the data says in week four. Enthusiasm peaks early; reliability problems surface late. Criteria written in advance protect you from your own honeymoon.

5. Scope creep mid-pilot. “While we’re at it, could it also…” is how a provable 30-day pilot becomes an unprovable 90-day project. New ideas are good news—they go on the roadmap, not into the pilot.

The success-criteria one-pager

Every pilot I run starts with a single page, agreed and signed before build day one. It has five rows:

Metric — the two or three numbers that define success. Almost always some combination of: time per occurrence, hours returned per week, turnaround time, and exception rate. Not “team satisfaction.” Numbers.

Baseline — what those numbers are today, actually measured. This week of measurement is the least glamorous and most valuable part of the whole exercise.

Target — what the automation must hit. Set it where the ROI math works, not at perfection. “Invoice processing under 2 minutes with fewer than 3% exceptions” is a target; “fully automated invoicing” is a fantasy that guarantees failure.

Measurement method — who records the numbers, where, how often. If measurement requires manual effort, it won’t happen; build the counting into the automation itself.

Decision rule — the sentence that prevents limbo: “On day 30 we review the scorecard. All targets hit → scale to full volume. Any target missed → stop, document why, decide fresh.” Agreed in advance, when everyone is calm and nobody has sunk costs to defend.

Two details that separate real pilots from theater

Exceptions are a design feature. A pilot targeting a 0% exception rate will either fail or lie. The right design routes the hard 5–10% of cases to a human review queue and counts them honestly. The automation’s job is to make the routine invisible, not to eliminate judgment.

A failed pilot must be cheap and informative. This is the whole point of piloting. If the pilot misses, you should walk away with the build, the documentation, and a written analysis of why—usually something upstream, like inconsistent intake data, that’s worth fixing regardless. If failure would be expensive or embarrassing, it wasn’t structured as a pilot; it was a launch wearing a pilot’s name tag.

None of this requires an enterprise procurement department. It requires one page, one measurement week, and the discipline to let the numbers—not the vibes—make the call. That discipline is cheap. Its absence is what’s expensive.
